Building a website without knowing what your prospective customers type into a search engine is like printing flyers and tossing them into an empty alley. Doing practical keyword research for small business websites ensures you build pages that answer real customer questions and attract people who actually want your services.

When small business owners ask me to fix an underperforming website, the problem is rarely design alone. Most often, the copy uses internal company jargon that nobody ever searches for. ## Why keyword research matters before writing any website copy

When you build a website for your business, it is tempting to jump straight into designing pages and writing slogans. However, guessing what your audience searches for almost always leads to wasted effort. You might call your service "bespoke residential climate control," while every homeowner in your area is searching for "emergency air conditioning repair."

Keyword research bridges the gap between what you sell and how customers ask for it. It reveals the exact language your audience uses, which prevents you from having to rewrite all your pages six months down the line. When you draft headings and paragraphs based on real search terms, your [on-page SEO checklist](../on-page-seo-checklist/) becomes much easier to execute.

Researching keywords also prevents you from building pages that have zero search demand. Every page on your site should serve a clear business purpose, whether that is answering a quick question or selling a specific service.

## Start with seed keywords from your daily customer conversations

The easiest way to find keywords for small business websites is to listen to the people already paying you. A seed keyword is simply a basic, two- or three-word phrase that describes your core product or service without any extra modifiers. Examples include "electrician," "leather boots," or "web design."

You do not need fancy software to build your first seed list. Look through your daily business interactions and note down the exact phrases people use:

- **Customer emails and inquiries:** Check what people type into your contact form or ask about over email.
- **Common phone questions:** What are the top three services people ask about when they call your front desk?
- **Past reviews and testimonials:** Look at how clients describe the problem you solved for them.
- **Competitor navigation menus:** Look at local competitors to see how they label their primary service pages.

If you serve a specific geographic region, local keyword research is essential at this stage. Add your city, neighborhood, or service areas next to your core terms (for example, "plumber in Lahore" or "commercial painter North Shore"). ## Use free tools to see what people actually type into Google

Once you have ten to fifteen seed phrases, you can expand them into full search queries. Many agency blogs claim you need an expensive monthly subscription to run keyword research. For a small business website, you can get reliable, accurate data using free keyword research tools.

Table: Free tools for small business keyword research
| Tool | What it does best | One limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Google Autocomplete** | Shows popular, real-time phrases as you type into Google | Does not show exact monthly search volume |
| **Google Search Console** | Shows queries bringing actual visitors to your existing site | Only works after your website has been running for a while |
| **Google Trends** | Compares seasonal interest and regional demand for terms | Better for broad topics than specific local service phrases |
| **Google Keyword Planner** | Gives estimated search ranges directly from Google | Requires creating a free Google Ads account |

Start by opening an incognito browser window and typing your seed keywords into the Google search box. Pay attention to the automatic suggestions that drop down. These are real search terms that people frequently type. 

After running a search, scroll down to the "People also ask" box and the "Related searches" section at the bottom of the results page. These sections give you long-tail keywords—longer, more specific phrases that often carry higher intent to buy than short, generic words.

:::callout Tip
When you search for your services in Google, look for patterns in the "People also ask" questions. These make excellent headings for your FAQ sections or blog posts.
:::

## How to check search intent so you do not waste effort

Search intent is the primary goal a person has when typing a phrase into Google. If you do not match what the searcher expects to find, Google will not rank your page, no matter how well-written it is.

Search queries usually fall into three main categories for a business site:

1. **Informational intent:** The user wants to learn something or fix a minor issue themselves (for example, "how to fix a leaking tap").
2. **Commercial intent:** The user is comparing solutions or providers (for example, "best accounting software for sole traders").
3. **Transactional intent:** The user is ready to hire someone or purchase a product immediately (for example, "hire emergency plumber near me").

Before you pick a keyword, type it into Google and look at the first five results. If all top results are detailed how-to blog posts, trying to rank a commercial sales page for that phrase will be an uphill battle. 

If the results show local business listings, service pages, or pricing tables, then creating a dedicated service page is the right move. If you run an e-commerce store, the same rule applies: ensure product-related queries lead to catalog pages rather than general articles. ## Organize keywords into primary and secondary targets for your pages

Once you have a list of twenty to fifty relevant terms, you need a plan for how to choose keywords for website pages. A common mistake is trying to stuff every keyword you found onto your home page. This confuses search engines and makes your copy difficult to read.

Instead, group your terms by topic and assign them to specific pages using a simple spreadsheet:

- **One primary keyword per page:** This is the main phrase that describes what the page is about. It usually goes in the page title, the main heading, and the meta description.
- **Two to four secondary keywords:** These are close variations, synonyms, or related subtopics that fit naturally into subheadings and body paragraphs.
- **A clear intent label:** Note down whether the page is a service landing page, a product page, or a blog post.

For example, if you run a bicycle repair shop, your primary keyword for a service page might be "bicycle tune up service." Your secondary keywords could be "bike brake adjustment," "gear cable replacement cost," and "annual bicycle maintenance."

## Frequently asked questions {#faq data-toc-label="FAQ"}

### How many keywords should I target on one page?
Aim for one primary keyword and two to four secondary keywords per page. Trying to target too many unrelated topics on a single page weakens your relevance and confuses search engines.

### Do I need to pay for premium SEO software as a small business?
No, free tools like Google Autocomplete, Google Search Console, and Google Keyword Planner provide plenty of reliable data for local and small business sites. Paid tools are helpful for large agencies, but they are not required to find profitable search terms.

### Can I target the same keyword on two different pages?
Avoid targeting the exact same primary keyword on multiple pages of your site. When you do this, your own pages compete against each other in search results, which can drag down the rankings for both.
