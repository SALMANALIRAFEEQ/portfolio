Many small business owners spend months publishing new blog posts and adding products, only to wonder why their pages struggle to rank on Google. In my work building and maintaining client sites, one of the most overlooked fixes is internal linking for SEO. Connecting related pages on your site costs nothing, requires no external outreach, and directly helps search engines understand what your business offers.

When you set up clear paths between your pages, you help both potential customers and search crawlers find your best content. In this guide, I will walk you through how internal links work, how to organize your site navigation, and a straightforward routine you can use every time you publish something new.

## What internal linking is and why it matters

An internal link is any hyperlink that points from one page on your domain to another page on the same domain. For example, when a link in your blog post points to your contact page or a specific service package, that is an internal link. This is different from an external link, which points to a completely different website.

Search engines like Google discover new content by crawling links across the web. When Google's bot lands on your homepage, it follows the links it finds there to reach your service pages, your about page, and your blog posts. If a page has no links pointing to it, search engines struggle to discover it, and visitors rarely find it. In web design, we call those unattended pages "orphan pages."

Internal links also pass ranking value—often called page authority—around your site. Your homepage usually collects the most attention and backlinks from other websites. When your homepage links directly to your key service pages, some of that value flows straight to them. Beyond search engines, clear links make your site easy for visitors to explore, which keeps them reading and moving closer to an inquiry or sale.

:::callout Key takeaway
Internal links act as a road map for your website. They show visitors where to go next and tell search engines which pages carry the most importance.
:::

## The main types of internal links on your website

Not every internal link serves the same purpose. When I work on client sites in WordPress, Shopify, or Webflow, I group links into three main categories:

### 1. Navigational links
These sit in your header menu and mobile menu. They give visitors a clear view of your top-level pages, such as "Services," "About," "Shop," and "Contact." Keep this menu clean and organized. Cramming twenty links into your main navigation confuses people and dilutes the structure of your site.

### 2. Contextual links
These are links placed naturally inside the body text of a page or article. For example, if you write a guide about home renovations and mention kitchen cabinet painting, you can link that phrase directly to your dedicated cabinet painting service page. Contextual links carry strong weight for SEO because they sit surrounded by relevant text that explains what the destination page is about.

### 3. Structural and footer links
These links live in your website footer, sidebars, breadcrumb trails, or "related posts" grids. A footer menu usually holds utility pages like your privacy policy, terms, shipping information, and direct links to main categories. Breadcrumbs—the small trail of links often seen at the top of an online store page (like *Home > Shoes > Running Shoes*)—help visitors quickly move back up a category hierarchy.

## How to choose descriptive anchor text

Anchor text is the clickable text that holds a hyperlink. When someone reads your page, the anchor text appears highlighted or underlined. Search engine crawlers use this text to understand what the target page is about before they even open it.

A common mistake I see on small business sites is using vague anchor text like "click here," "read more," or "learn more." These phrases give search engines zero context. If you link the words "click here" fifty times across your website, Google learns nothing about the pages receiving those links.

Here is how to pick better anchor text:

- **Be specific:** Instead of writing "To see our pricing, [click here]," write "You can review our [web development pricing and packages] on our services page."
- **Keep it natural:** The sentence should read smoothly out loud. Do not force awkward keyword phrases just to match a search term.
- **Vary your phrases:** If you have five blog posts linking to your plumbing repair page, do not use the exact phrase "emergency plumbing repair" every single time. Mix it up naturally with phrases like "local plumbing repair services" or "fixing burst pipes."
- **Avoid whole-paragraph links:** Link a concise phrase of two to five words. Turning an entire sentence or paragraph into a giant blue link makes text hard to read.

> Good anchor text tells the reader exactly what they will see before they click the link.

## A simple step-by-step internal linking workflow

You do not need complicated enterprise software to build a solid internal link structure. Here is a practical workflow you can follow whenever you update your website or publish a new article:

1. **Map out your core pages:** Identify your money pages first. These are your main services, product categories, or consultation booking pages. These pages should receive the highest number of internal links from your informational content.
2. **Group related content into clusters:** If you run an e-commerce store selling outdoor gear, group your articles around broad topics like camping gear, hiking boots, and tent maintenance. Articles within the camping cluster should regularly link to one another and up to your camping product category.
3. **Link forward when you write a new post:** Whenever you draft a new post, include two to four links to existing articles or service pages on your site. For instance, if you write about managing product stock, it makes sense to link to a guide on [Shopify inventory management](../shopify-inventory-management-guide/) or [WooCommerce order management](../woocommerce-order-management-guide/).
4. **Link backward from older posts:** This is the step most people skip. After publishing a new article, open two or three older, related blog posts and add a link pointing forward to your new post. This ensures your new page gets discovered quickly by search engines.
5. **Use site search to find linking opportunities:** To quickly find older posts where you can place a link, use Google's site search operator. Type `site:yourdomain.com "target topic"` into Google. This shows you every page on your site where you have already mentioned that topic, giving you instant candidates for new contextual links.

:::callout Tip
Set a reminder once a quarter to review your top ten most visited pages in Google Search Console. Check whether those popular pages link down to your newer articles or key conversion pages.
:::

## Common internal linking mistakes to avoid

Internal linking is easy to manage once you get into the habit, but a few bad habits can undermine your SEO for small business efforts:

- **Creating orphan pages:** Every page you want people to find should have at least one or two internal links pointing to it. If you publish a page and never link to it from your menu, footer, or blog, search engines may take weeks to index it, or miss it completely.
- **Overloading a single page with links:** Linking out to thirty different pages from a 400-word blog post overwhelms the reader and looks spammy. Stick to links that genuinely help someone understand the topic better.
- **Linking to broken URLs:** When you change a page URL or delete an old post without setting up a redirect, any existing internal links pointing to that address will lead visitors to a 404 error page. A periodic check with a free site crawler helps you catch and fix broken links quickly.
- **Using identical anchor text for two different pages:** If you have one page about "Commercial Cleaning" and another about "Office Cleaning," do not use the anchor text "office cleaning" to point to both. This confuses search engines about which page is the authority for that term.
- **Relying solely on automated related-post plugins:** Automated plugins in [WordPress](https://wordpress.org/) can add a simple "related articles" block at the end of a post, but they do not replace hand-picked, contextual links placed inside your paragraphs.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq data-toc-label="FAQ"}

### How many internal links should I put on each page?
There is no fixed rule, but for a standard 1,000-word blog post, three to six natural contextual links are usually plenty. Focus on linking to pages that truly help the reader explore the topic further rather than trying to hit a specific number.

### Can having too many internal links hurt my SEO?
Yes, if you pack dozens of links into a short piece of content, it creates a poor reading experience and can look like spam to search engines. Keep your links helpful and spread out through your body text.

### Should internal links open in a new tab or the same tab?
Internal links should almost always open in the same browser tab so visitors can use their browser's "Back" button normally. Opening internal links in new tabs clutters the visitor's screen and disrupts their navigation flow.
